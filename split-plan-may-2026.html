The Split Plan
May 2026

Sir David Attenborough Hive × Carol Klein Hive — a documented plan for Ian & Pete

🌸

An overwintered Buckfast nucleus, arrived mid-April 2026, has built to 11–12 frames of active brood by the first week of May. The colony is at the threshold of swarm impulse. This is the plan to split it into two strong colonies before it swarms, calibrated to East Ayrshire conditions and informed by six independent AI models acting as experienced beekeepers.

🐝 Buckfast Colony 🌸 May · East Ayrshire 📋 6-Model Consensus
🔍
Saturday 9th May 2026
Pre-Split Inspection
Confirm readiness, check for queen cells
Actions
  • Full inspection — suit, smoker, hive tool. Work methodically frame by frame
  • Count frames with active brood — you need 10+ brood frames before splitting
  • Check every frame for queen cells — open cups, charged cells with larvae, capped cells. The presence of capped cells means the colony may already be committed to swarming
  • Confirm the queen is present and laying — look for eggs and young larvae
  • Assess stores — both hives will need reserves post-split
  • If capped queen cells found: do not proceed to Thursday split without reassessing the plan
⚠️
Swarm Risk
At 11–12 frames in early May at 55°N, this colony is at or near swarm impulse. Inspection this Saturday is not optional — it determines whether Thursday's split is safe to proceed as planned.
📖 Evidence & reasoning
▾
All 6 models GPT Gemini

All six models consulted agreed that a colony of this strength in early May at 55°N is in the swarm risk zone. GPT noted explicitly: "given 11–12 frames with brood across most frames, you are at or just before swarm impulse."

Gemini was the most urgent: "11–12 frames of brood in early May is massive. You are likely less than a week away from the colony making their own reproductive swarm decision."

GPT flagged that if capped queen cells are found, the colony may already be committed to swarming and the split plan needs to be reassessed rather than proceeding unchanged.

All models recommended inspecting for queen cells immediately before the split and acting on what is found rather than proceeding blindly.

✂️
Thursday 14th May 2026
Split Day
Divide the colony — Sir David stays, Carol Klein receives the split
🏠
Hive Positions
Sir David Attenborough Hive stays on the original site with the queen (~60% of frames). Carol Klein Hive goes 50cm to the right — queenless, receives ~40% of frames. Returning foragers come back to the original position and strengthen Carol Klein over time.
Before you open anything
  • Carol Klein Hive fully assembled on its stand, frames in, dummy board ready, feeder prepared with cooled 1:1 syrup
  • Both hives have distinct visual markers — different shape or colour on each, painted and dry before today
  • Suit on, smoker lit and producing cool white smoke before you approach
  • Fresh grass to hand for entrance plug
  • Hive record book open — Pete on note-taking, Ian on frames. Roles agreed before opening
The split sequence
  • Find the queen first. She stays in Sir David Attenborough Hive. Note which frame she's on
  • Give Sir David ~6–7 frames: the queen's frame, mixed brood in all stages, stores. This is the queenright 60%
  • Move ~5 frames into Carol Klein: brood in all stages (eggs AND larvae essential), stores, pollen. This is the queenless 40%
  • Shake extra nurse bees from Sir David frames into Carol Klein before closing — compensates for the foragers she'll lose to the original position
  • Dummy board tight against the last frame in Carol Klein
  • Close Carol Klein. Plug the entrance loosely with fresh grass — forces bees to push out slowly over 24–48 hours, compelling reorientation to new position. Reduces drift.
  • Reduce both entrances to minimum setting
  • Angle entrances very slightly differently — even a few degrees helps bees distinguish between hives
  • Close Sir David. Walk away.
  • Write up the split record — which frames went where, queen confirmed on which frame, time, weather
🚫
Do Not Feed Carol Klein Today
With a flow on in May, feeding the queenless split risks triggering robbing from Sir David. Carol Klein has stores on her frames. Leave her to manage until after queen introduction.
📖 Evidence & reasoning — hive positions
▾
GPT Gemini Perplexity

GPT, Gemini, and Perplexity all recommended placing the queenless split in the original position and moving the queen. Their reasoning: returning foragers bolster the queenless half, compensating for its lack of a laying queen. GPT: "you want that flight force to bolster the queenless unit, which otherwise has no laying queen and needs immediate intake to stabilise."

However, Mistral, Grok, and Deepseek made the opposite recommendation — queen stays on original site — arguing this keeps the established producing colony undisturbed and the queenless split gets nurse bees regardless.

South Acre decision: Queen stays in Sir David on the original site. This is the simpler operation for a first-season split and keeps the known productive queen undisturbed. Carol Klein gets nurse bees with the brood. Both approaches are established practice — this choice suits the South Acre context.

The grass entrance plug is Grok's recommendation, widely used in same-apiary splits to force reorientation and reduce drift when hives cannot be moved miles apart.

The 60/40 split is Grok and Deepseek's recommendation for northern latitudes where cold snaps remain a risk — the larger queenright half weathers weather disruption more reliably.

Saturday 16th May 2026
Destroy Cells · Introduce Queen
48 hours queenless — destroy all emergency cells, cage introduction
⚡
Critical Step — No Exceptions
Every single queen cell in Carol Klein must be found and destroyed before the queen is introduced. Missing even one risks the colony killing the introduced queen in favour of their own. Shake bees off every frame. Check every frame. Then check again.
Destroying queen cells — Carol Klein only
  • Suit on, smoker lit before you approach
  • Open Carol Klein Hive. Work methodically frame by frame
  • Shake bees off each frame — you need to see the wax surface clearly
  • Destroy every queen cell — open cups with larvae, capped cells, any peanut-shaped structure on bottom or sides of frames. Use your hive tool or a finger. Every single one.
  • Replace each frame in order as you check it
  • When all frames checked and cleared — confirm no queen cells remain visible
Introducing the queen
  • Take the queen cage from indoor storage. Check she is still alive and moving
  • Identify two central brood frames — nurse bees here are most receptive to a new queen
  • Place the cage between the two brood frames, candy end slightly downward so dead attendants cannot block the exit
  • Mesh side facing outward so bees can access and feed her through the mesh
  • Leave the fondant tab in place — do not expose the candy yet. This happens Monday.
  • Close the hive. Do not open again until Monday.
  • Write up the record — time, weather, number of queen cells destroyed, queen introduced
👀
Reading Acceptance
Before removing the tab on Monday, look at the bees on the outside of the cage through the roof gap. Calm bees feeding her through the mesh = good sign. Bees biting and pulling at the mesh aggressively = rejection risk, wait another 24 hours before exposing candy.
📖 Evidence & reasoning — queenless period and introduction method
▾
4-model consensus GPT Grok Deepseek Perplexity

Queenless period — 24–48 hours: Four of the six models recommended this window. GPT: the period "dissipates residual queen pheromone so acceptance improves, but still precedes the point where they have committed strongly to emergency queen rearing."

Gemini recommended immediate introduction (same day) which is the highest-rejection-risk option and was set aside. The supplier's day-8 method is the most conservative and reliable for acceptance, but requires finding every single queen cell in a populous colony twice — a significant challenge for first-season beekeepers. The 24–48 hour window is the practical consensus.

Candy end downward: Gemini's specific recommendation — prevents dead attendant bees from blocking the exit and potentially trapping the queen.

Destroy ALL cells: The supplier, GPT, and Perplexity all emphasise this without exception. GPT: "missed queen cells in the queenless half → virgin emerges and kills introduced queen." Shake bees off every frame to see the wax surface clearly.

Tab in place for 48 hours: Slow release is more reliable than immediate candy exposure. The bees spend 48 hours acclimatising to her pheromones before they begin releasing her. All six models recommended slow release over direct release.

Monday 18th May 2026
Remove Fondant Tab
48 hours after introduction — expose the candy, let release begin
Actions
  • Suit on. Approach Carol Klein calmly
  • Lift roof only — do not do a full inspection
  • Observe the bees on the cage if visible — calm and feeding = proceed. Aggressive balling = wait 24 more hours
  • If calm: remove the plastic tab covering the fondant to expose the candy
  • Replace the cage between the frames exactly as it was
  • Roof on. Do not open the hive again until Saturday 23rd.
⏳
What Happens Now
The bees will eat through the fondant over the next 2–4 days, slowly releasing the queen. She'll emerge into a colony that has been living with her pheromones for several days and is far more likely to accept her as their own. This is the crucial window — leave them completely undisturbed.
📖 Evidence & reasoning
▾
Supplier method GPT Gemini

The supplier's introduction guide specifies: "Day 10: Leave the sealed cage for 48hrs. This allows the bees to get used to the smell of their new queen. After 48hrs you can remove the plastic tab to expose the fondant."

Gemini's note on cage behaviour is worth acting on: if bees are calm and feeding her through the mesh, acceptance is proceeding well. If they are aggressively biting or balling the cage, wait another 24 hours before exposing the candy — forced release into a hostile colony is the primary cause of queen rejection.

The 5–7 day hands-off period after tab removal is a consensus recommendation across all models — repeated early inspections are the second most common cause of rejection after missed queen cells.

🥚
Saturday 23rd May 2026
First Post-Introduction Check
Look for eggs — the only confirmation that matters
🥚
What You're Looking For
Eggs — not the queen. Eggs confirm she has been released, accepted, and is laying. They are tiny, upright, standing in the base of cells like grains of rice. Good light, sun over your shoulder, a loupe if needed. You don't need to see the queen herself.
Carol Klein Hive inspection
  • Suit on, smoker lit. Check weather — above 14°C, dry, calm
  • Remove the empty queen cage — she will have been released. Note any candy remaining
  • Look for eggs on the central brood frames — work methodically, take your time
  • Destroy any queen cells present — the colony may still have one or two. Remove them all.
  • Assess frame coverage — are bees occupying more frames than at split?
  • Check stores — feed 1:1 syrup if light, especially if the June gap is beginning
  • Write up the record
Sir David Attenborough Hive inspection
  • Full inspection while you're suited up — check for swarm cells, brood pattern, stores
  • A colony that has just been split can still build swarm pressure — monitor carefully
  • Add a super if approaching 7–8 frames covered solidly
🔍
If No Eggs Seen
Don't panic. The supplier notes it can take 2–3 weeks for a caged queen to come back into full lay after introduction stress. If no eggs at day 7, wait another 7 days before reassessing. Only if no eggs after 3 weeks should you consider the introduction has failed and contact the supplier.
📖 Evidence & reasoning — post-introduction monitoring
▾
All 6 models Supplier

All six models recommended checking for eggs rather than the queen as the primary confirmation of successful introduction. GPT: "confirm release and look for eggs, not the queen."

The supplier explicitly notes: "After 1 week, we go back and check for eggs to see if the queen has been successfully introduced. If you see eggs, then there is no need to see the queen. It can take 2–3 weeks for mated queens to come back into lay following being caged."

June gap: Three models flagged the potential late-May/early-June nectar dearth between spring and summer flows. In Scotland this can cause a temporary interruption to foraging. Monitor stores in both colonies and be prepared to feed lightly if needed.

Swarm monitoring continues: GPT noted that the queenright half can still build swarm cells even after a split, especially in a strong spring flow. Maintain 7-day inspection intervals through June for both hives.
